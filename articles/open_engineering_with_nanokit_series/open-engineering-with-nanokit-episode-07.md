## Episode 7: Choosing the Package

Every build-out eventually reaches the question that cannot be answered by architecture diagrams alone: which package actually gets used to launch. Nano Kit offers three, LiteWave, Turbo Mode, and Pixel Pro, and the right one depends less on budget than on how much of the Episode 1 through 6 plan needs to ship at once.

### SIPOC

| Supplier | Input | Process | Output | Customer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nano Kit | LiteWave (free), Turbo Mode, Pixel Pro packages | Compare each package against the launch scope: single homepage versus full nine-subdomain rollout | A chosen package matching the current milestone | Whoever maintains open-engineering.io day to day |
| Ecosystem architect | The recommended next milestone: designing the homepage itself | Scope the very first release narrowly around communicating what, why, and how | A homepage-first launch plan | www.open-engineering.io visitors |
| Site maintainer | Chosen package, live site | Decide when to upgrade as docs, platform, and registry neighborhoods come online | A staged upgrade path tied to the domain rollout from Episode 5 | Future contributors joining a growing site |

### Starting where the milestone actually is

The next milestone identified for Open Engineering is not the whole nine-subdomain map, it is the homepage: a landing page that immediately communicates what Open Engineering is, why it exists, and how the ecosystem is organized, guiding visitors naturally toward platform, operating systems, and applications. That scope fits comfortably inside Nano Kit's most popular tier, Turbo Mode, without needing to commit to Pixel Pro before docs.open-engineering.io or platform.open-engineering.io even exist.

### Growing into the package, not the other way around

As the subdomains from Episode 5 come online one at a time, and as custom components from Episode 6 start replacing generated sections, the case for a higher package grows alongside the site itself. There is no need to buy for the nine-neighborhood future on day one; the packages exist precisely so the choice can scale with the rollout instead of forcing it ahead of schedule.

### The build, closed

From a blank canvas that refused to fight a small black logo, through an AI-described hero built on the platform's own architecture diagram, a navigation tree pulled straight from GitHub, README content moved rather than rewritten, a staged map of subdomains, and an export path that keeps the door open for custom components, Open Engineering's homepage is now a scoped, buildable milestone rather than an open-ended ambition. The architecture was already there. Nano Kit's job was only ever to give it a front door.
